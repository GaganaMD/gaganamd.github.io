Research direction

Three questions I want to pursue.

For prospective supervisors: each question comes from a finished project and ends with the experiment I'd run next.

1. When does a monitor buy enough time to act?

Why. Control protocols are usually scored on whether a monitor eventually flags misbehaviour. That's not the same as whether anyone could still have stopped it.

So far. In Detection Is Not Containment, detection was timely by exactly one step, and it depended on how identifiers were written. A false alarm had a measurable cost: a benign task failed.

Next. The same timing measurements across several environments with different authorization structures, with sampled monitor outputs and reported variance. Benign intervention cost reported next to prevention rate.

2. How much do audit conclusions depend on what the auditor can see?

Why. Auditors usually work from logs that were reconstructed, summarized or partly lost. Claims about an agent's objectives inherit those gaps.

So far. In two collusion studies, behaviour overstated what we could conclude about intent: agents tracked a colluding peer's behaviour but never noticed it had been swapped out (0 of 468 probes), and a pricing LLM's high prices had a steerable internal trace but no punishment logic. Work on hidden loyalties and on native versus reconstructed traces is ongoing.

Next. Hold the agent fixed, degrade the evidence in controlled ways (drop tool events, summarize, reorder), and measure which audit conclusions survive.

3. When can we trust an environment a model wrote?

Why. LLMs increasingly write the simulations that agents are trained and tested in. Safety reasoning about reachability or reversibility is only as good as that environment.

So far. Generated games almost always run (134 of 135), but most don't encode the intended structure (mean structural F1 0.366). Naming a country in the prompt changed the structure produced by four of five models.

Next. Cheap structural checks that could flag a wrong environment before it's used, validated against the reference games we already have.

AI control · Monitoring

Detection Is Not Containment

Measures whether a monitor flags a prohibited action early enough for an intervention to prevent it, then replays the intervention to measure both prevention and the cost of false alarms. This project also absorbed my earlier work on intervention-aware monitoring: revoking a capability at the moment of detection.

My part: I built and released the monitoring experiment (Phases A–F): the environment, frozen oracle, monitor views, prefix-timing metrics, invariance diagnostics, closed-loop replay, tests and release checks. Kaustubh Gupta built the incident-forensics and checklist pipeline. I'm first author on the report.

Method, results and limits
Method
Synthetic authorization environment. Three monitor views (actions; plus reasoning; plus environment telemetry). Local qwen3:14b monitor with a frozen prompt and oracle. Threshold calibrated on benign controls only. Preregistered follow-up phases.
Result
Grounded detection was timely by one step (margin +1). Decisions held in 8 of 12 meaning-preserving identifier renamings. In a closed-loop replay, a false alarm revoked a benign agent's authority and failed its task.
Limits
One environment, single deterministic draws, fixed prompts. Says nothing about chain-of-thought faithfulness or general control performance.

Multi-agent safety · Collusion

What can we infer about colluding agents from their behaviour?

Two projects from Apart Research's AI Collusion Research Sprint (October 2026). One asks whether colluding agents notice when a partner is swapped out; the other asks whether high prices set by an LLM pricing agent are actually evidence of a collusive strategy. Both come back to the same caution: what an agent does is easier to observe than why it does it.

My part: On the pricing project, I co-led the project direction and experimental setup with Sriram Bharadwaj and ran the mechanistic interpretability and steering experiments (probes, activation patching, steering against random-direction controls). On the peer-swap project, I led the RepeatedJira experiment, in which a monitor covertly takes over a colluder's seat, and the experiment code lives in my CollusionTrace repository.

Method, results and limits
Peer swaps
Noticing the Move, Missing the Player. In a Diner's Dilemma, a Public Goods Game and a RepeatedJira collusion setup, agents tracked behaviour, not identity. In the Public Goods Game, 0 of 468 probes named a silent swap, while free riding was clearly detected (AUC 0.915–0.984). A monitor that covertly took over a colluder's seat disrupted the collusion, but the remaining agent never suspected a replacement. With Siva Kumar Lakkoju and Kaustubh Gupta.
Tacit pricing
Tacit Collusion in Pricing Agents. One fixed, competitive agent broke a collusive pricing habit more cheaply than adding competitors, for Q-learning agents and for Qwen2.5-7B. Inside the model, the rival's price is linearly decodable, activation patching locates where it acts, and a steering vector moves the model's price far more than norm-matched random directions. But a pre-registered study (1,656 runs) found no punishment of undercutting: the model reacts to any change in the rival's price. With Chewon Cho and Sriram Bharadwaj.
Limits
48-hour sprint reports, not peer-reviewed. Small open models; the authors flag that more capable and reasoning models may behave differently.

World models · Evaluation

Do LLM-generated world models keep their structure?

If an LLM writes the environment an agent is tested in, and the environment quietly changes which states are reachable or which actions exist, any safety conclusion drawn inside it inherits that error. We tested whether LLMs can build multi-agent situations as executable partially observable stochastic games (POSGs).

My part: Main contributor to the shared codebase (78 of about 116 non-merge commits): experiment runners and preserved run artifacts, the graph-analysis library, the test suite, and parts of the paper source. Teammates contributed scenario data, reports, JSON generation and prompts.

Method, results and limits
Method
A 44-scenario benchmark across toy, situational, game and cultural settings. Five LLMs, three generations per model and scenario. Three evaluation lanes: mechanical validity, permutation-invariant structural matching against reference games, and qualitative coverage. Every run keeps its prompts, raw replies, generated code and validation reports.
Result
410 first-pass generations across three tracks. In the game track, 134 of 135 passed a strict mechanical check, but the mean permutation-invariant structural F1 was 0.366 (median 0.18), with 28 of 134 exact matches. Repeated generations varied (within-pair SD 0.17). In the cultural track, naming the country in a norm-conflict prompt changed the generated structure for four of five models, while automated semantic scores barely moved. Executability is necessary but not sufficient: a model that runs can still hand downstream planning and neuro-symbolic reasoning the wrong structure.
Limits
Reference structures exist only for the nine games. The link between a game's stochasticity and lower agreement (ρ = −0.68) is exploratory, from nine games.
Accepted · NeurIPS 2026 NEmo workshop PRISM AI Safety Research Fellowship, May – Oct 2026 (completed). Mentored by Jobst Heitzig. Signal processing · Deep learning

Hyperspectral BiorNet: a complete wavelet framework for unmixing

Each pixel of a hyperspectral image mixes the spectra of several materials. Unmixing recovers the pure spectra (endmembers) and their proportions (abundances), under the linear mixing model x ≈ E a, with a ≥ 0 and Σa = 1. The problem is ill-posed: there are more unknowns than observations, and noise and spectral variability blur the signatures.

BiorNet does the whole thing in the wavelet domain. A biorthogonal discrete wavelet transform splits each pixel's spectrum into approximation and detail coefficients, and the endmembers and abundances are learned from those. The network's nonlinearity is a wavelet as well: a custom biorthogonal-inspired activation,

φ(x) = max(0, x) · sin(5x) · e−x²/2

This is a Gaussian-windowed oscillation, so the activation has the same localised, oscillatory shape as the basis the data is expressed in. A ReLU can't respond that way. We evaluated it on synthetic data and the HYDICE Urban benchmark against state-of-the-art unmixing models, using RMSE, spectral angle distance and spectral information divergence.

My part: first author. I worked on the wavelet framework and the design of the activation function, and built the implementation.

Published · IEEE InGARSS 2025

Optimisation · Edge–fog systems

Co-scheduling UAV routes and analytics across edge and fog

Drones used for crop monitoring and inspection need to run analytics such as DNN inference on what they capture. In rural areas there is little connectivity to offload that work. The idea here is to use rural transit buses, which increasingly carry compute and Wi-Fi hotspots, as mobile fog nodes. A drone can hand data to a passing bus, and the bus can compute on it or forward it once it reaches network coverage.

We formulated this as a Mission Scheduling Problem: choose drone flight paths that cover every task waypoint, and co-schedule each analytics task on board, on a static fog node, or on a bus, in line with bus timetables and task deadlines. Our Divide and Assign heuristic trades optimality for speed on large instances and is compared against the optimal solution. On real-world data it achieves 31–230% higher average utility than baselines and the state of the art, completes 100% of tasks across all workloads, and runs on the order of 10⁵ seconds faster than the optimal solver.

A secondary thread was reinforcement learning for split inference: deciding where to cut a model's layers between devices (ExpansionNet v2, YOLOv5).

Paper: Suman Raj, Arindam Khanda, Gagana M D, Yogesh Simmhan, Sajal K. Das. Mobility-Aware Cost-Efficient Co-Scheduling of UAV Routes and Analytics across Edge and Fog Computing.

Research internship · IISc DREAM Lab Jan – Aug 2025. DREAM Lab, led by Yogesh Simmhan.
Reinforcement learning · Behaviour

The Matching Law in transformer-based RL agents

Animals split their choices between options in proportion to how rewarding each one is (Herrnstein's Matching Law). Does a transformer-based RL agent do the same? We trained agents on a two-choice task cued by MNIST digits, varying the reward ratio from 1:1 to 16:1, and compared response ratios with reward ratios on log–log axes. The agents' choice proportions closely tracked the reward ratios, with an approximately linear log–log relationship.

My part: I worked on the experiment design and methodology and ran the experiments, with Yagmur Karakoc, Emad Sharifi and Zahra Sarayloo.

Representations · Geometry

Latent manifold reasoning: when does a reasoning trajectory collapse?

Think of reasoning as a smooth path through a latent manifold. Forcing that path through a narrow, discrete interface (text, a tool call, a serialised state) can make it collapse: neighbouring states stop being neighbours, and the path takes detours the original never did. I built a sandbox to make that idea measurable. It routes trajectories through PCA embeddings of the scikit-learn digits data under a bottleneck of increasing strength, and tracks path distortion, k-nearest-neighbour preservation and excess path length.

Early versions had no baselines, and almost any perturbation looked like collapse. Later versions compare structured routing against isotropic noise and shuffled anchors, with bootstrap intervals and permutation tests. The result that held up: structured routing collapses local neighbourhood structure far more than noise does, while shortening the path. One geometric metric can look better while the trajectory becomes less faithful. This is a toy setting, not evidence about transformer hidden states.

My part: independent project. All eight versions are kept, so the method changes are visible.

Interpretability · Causal reasoning

Prompt sensitivity and latent drift in causal reasoning

If you reword a causal-reasoning question but keep its causal structure, does a small model's answer flip, and do the flips line up with movement in its hidden states? I built synthetic vignettes covering confounding, mediation and selection bias, with variants that remove a confounder or rename terms. On Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct I ran prompt-stability analysis, layerwise probes and activation contrasts. Early runs showed high answer flip rates across reframings, with the largest drift under "mechanistic" prompts. Probe separability and drift are correlational, and the repository says so.

My part: independent project, built to experiment with causal interpretation. Every run saves its config and a run manifest.
